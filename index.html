Fly a fictional Pathfinder spaceplane over real Earth imagery. The first Earth now includes measured terrain, an Alpine reference flight, and a global flight planner. Cities and buildings come later.

Altitude and clearance: MSL is the aircraft reference point above the simulator's sea-level datum. MODEL CLEARANCE is the signed radial gap at the modeled belly/gear probes, using collision terrain; it is not a surveyed radar measurement and does not include trees or wingtip contact. Nearby published summit heights are shown separately. For example, 10 m above Everest's 8,848.86 m reference reads 8,858.9 m MSL and +10.0 m above that reference, even if the terrain model is lower. Low clearance and known summit proximity trigger warnings; the terrain look-ahead samples a constant-velocity path for up to eight seconds, so turns and acceleration can invalidate it. Critical warnings remain visible in clean view. Caspian water uses a fixed −28 m historical reference, not a live level.

Game controls: W climbs, S dives; A/D command a banked turn and ease back toward wings level when released. Hold Shift for boost and Ctrl for the airbrake. Shift + = and Shift + − still set full/cut base throttle; Z, X and C fire the gun, launch a missile and lock a target; N drops a nuclear bomb, so turn away and get at least 10 km clear before the blast wave arrives. Game mode limits engine demand near the pressure ceiling, but cannot prevent every dive, stall, terrain collision or torch overload. Direct mode retains S-up/W-down, manual roll and Shift/Ctrl throttle adjustment. Mouse mode uses a spring-centred stick: hold left mouse and drag to steer; release to centre. Right-drag looks around. Gamepad triggers boost/brake in Game mode.

Navigation: the compass tape shows true heading. The moving map follows the aircraft and records the latest 30 minutes of flight. Use +/− or the mouse wheel to zoom, drag to pan, Follow to recenter, and ↗ to enlarge. Double click a location to plan a flight. The Sun readout shows geometric azimuth/elevation at your coordinates and the simulation UTC; no atmospheric refraction or terrain shadow correction is included.

Power: normal drive peaks at a fictional 0.65 g, boost at 1.5 g, and the orbital torch at 30 g. Engines spool gradually; the torch is reduced in dense airflow. Pitch rate decreases with airspeed. Releasing pitch trims angle of attack rather than freezing height, so a climb or descent continues until you change the flight path.

Feel the flight: the centred, horizon-stable chase camera, wider speed-dependent field of view, air-relative streaks and gentle transonic buffet provide motion cues. Motion off disables the streaks, vapor and camera shake; reduced-motion preferences start with those effects disabled.

Mach run: choose Mach run · 11 km, click Start, then hold Shift. Watch the meter cross Mach 1; a restrained wing vapor collar can appear in the modeled moist lower air. The HUD announces the crossing. A short bipolar pressure-wave cue plays in chase view if sound is enabled; cockpit view does not play your own boom. Hold Ctrl to brake. The cue fires once per crossing, with hysteresis, and never for vacuum orbital speeds. It is game feedback: real booms occur when a supersonic shock passes an observer, not just at the instant the aircraft passes Mach 1. Condensation depends on humidity; the game vapor is stylized, not live weather. NASA sonic boom explanation.

First takeoff: choose Sahara surface, click Start, and hold R. Pitch up with W (S in Direct mode) and add throttle to climb. Torch gives a fictional 30 g drive; use it above the atmosphere.

Landing: try Alpine slope · landing approach, or press V below 2 km at low speed. It brakes drift, lowers the gear and aligns with gentle terrain near touchdown. On ground steeper than 12°, it attempts to hold above the site instead of descending. Choose another site before fuel runs out. Manual touchdown needs gear, under 6 m/s normal speed, under 5 m/s surface drift, attitude within 8° of the terrain normal, and slope at most 12°. The three pads must fit within 0.75 m of simulated gear travel. These are fictional vehicle limits, not real landing guidance. Ocean contact is a splashdown, not a landing.

Space and speed: crossing the 100 km Kármán line needs altitude, not one mandatory speed. A powered climb can cross it slowly. Staying in orbit needs sideways velocity: about 7.67 km/s at 400 km, for a 92-minute orbit. Escape speed is about 11.2 km/s near sea level, ignoring air. Your fictional 30 g torch can reach both; use it in thin air or vacuum. Turning the nose in vacuum does not turn the trajectory.

A fast trip around Earth: choose Low Earth orbit, cut thrust, then select 100× time for roughly a minute per circuit. This speeds up the simulation clock. A circuit is relative to inertial space; Earth rotates beneath you. Low-altitude circuits require powered flight and continuous terrain clearance. Orbital speeds in dense air exceed the airframe's pressure and heating limits. There is no safe ground-skimming orbit.

Orbital values use Earth-centred inertial velocity and assume no air, terrain or thrust. The air-load limit uses airspeed and is a continuous structural envelope. Excess dynamic pressure or aerodynamic load above 12 g accumulates damage; short excursions do not instantly destroy the aircraft. Heating and terrain still impose limits.

Atmosphere layers: troposphere (0–12 km), stratosphere (12–50 km), mesosphere (50–85 km), thermosphere (85–700 km), exosphere (700–10,000 km). These are approximate boundaries, which vary with location and conditions. Space begins by convention at 100 km, within the thermosphere: the air does not abruptly stop there. Beyond the exosphere, Earth's outer gas envelope still fades gradually. The ionosphere overlaps several layers.

Colours as you climb: low-altitude haze fades into deeper blue, then a black overhead sky with a thin blue Earth limb. Sun angle changes the haze at sunrise and sunset. The layers do not have separate coloured shells. Ground colours follow geographic imagery, forest coverage and terrain elevation; climbing changes their atmospheric appearance.

Terrain during descent: finer elevation loads ahead of low flight. Incoming changes wait for the matching visual mesh, then the visible and collision surfaces blend together. Updates remain held near the ground to protect clearance. If the terrain readout says an update is held, climb above 1.5 km clearance before descending again. The Alpine reference flights include a finer offline elevation set. Surface imagery remains coarser than elevation; small rocks and trees are not collision objects.

Scientific references: NASA atmospheric layers, NASA atmosphere and space boundary, NASA orbital and escape speeds.
